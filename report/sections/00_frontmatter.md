# GPUs and TPUs for LLM Training: A Technical and Commercial Field Guide

**Compiled 20 August 2026.** This is a self-contained reference on the hardware that trains and serves large language models: how GPUs and TPUs work, why they are built differently, what every current accelerator's specifications actually are, what they cost, who can get them, and how they compare on compute, memory, interconnect, efficiency and price.

Every figure in this document is generated by code in `scripts/` from the datasets in `data/`, so any number can be traced, re-plotted, or corrected.

---

## How to read this report

Three conventions matter enough to state up front, because most published accelerator comparisons get them wrong.

**1. Dense, not sparse.** NVIDIA, AMD and Meta headline their tensor throughput *with 2:1 structured sparsity*, which is exactly 2× the dense rate and requires 2:4-pruned weights that production LLM training does not use. Every FLOPS number in this report is **dense** — vendor headline figures have been halved where the asterisk applies. The most honest public comment on this comes from Meta's own ISCA 2025 paper on MTIA: 2:4 sparsity shipped in the hardware, but "our production experience indicates that exploiting sparsity is challenging due to potential quality loss… this feature is not yet widely used in production." Read every "with sparsity" figure in the industry through that sentence.

**2. Confidence is labelled.** Accelerator economics is an evidence-poor field: NVIDIA, AMD and Google publish no list prices for datacenter parts, no supplier breaks out HBM revenue, and no lab publishes training compute any more. Claims here carry one of three tags.

| Tag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **confirmed** | Stated by the party in a position to know — vendor datasheet, SEC filing, earnings call, official price list, peer-reviewed paper, MLCommons result |
| **estimate** | Published by a named analyst, bank or trade publication, or derived arithmetically in this report from confirmed inputs |
| **rumor** | Single-source channel check or anonymously sourced report; included only where nothing better exists, and flagged |

**3. Peak FLOPS is an upper bound you will not reach.** Well-tuned dense pretraining lands at 35–50% model FLOPs utilisation. Inference decode at batch 32 is structurally capped near 11% of an H100's peak, no matter how good the kernels are. Any comparison based on peak numbers overstates differences that real workloads compress, and hides differences — memory capacity, fabric bandwidth, software maturity — that real workloads amplify.

## The short version

If you read nothing else, these are the load-bearing conclusions.

**On architecture.** A GPU is a general-purpose parallel machine that hides memory latency dynamically with warp switching and hardware caches. A TPU is a matrix machine whose data movement is placed statically by a compiler, with scratchpads instead of caches and no thread-level scheduling at all. The GPU pays silicon and timing variance for the ability to run any kernel; the TPU trades flexibility for MACs per watt and for near-deterministic step times, which is what makes 9,216-chip synchronous training practical. Every serious non-GPU design now shipping — TPU, Trainium, Maia 200, Groq, Cerebras, MTIA — has independently converged on software-managed on-chip memory.

**On what actually changed recently.** Not FLOPS. The scale-up domain: the number of chips inside one coherent, uniformly high-bandwidth pool went from 8 (HGX, a decade-long constraint) to 72 (GB200/GB300 NVL72, AMD Helios) to 9,216 (a TPU v7 superpod). That single number decides whether tensor parallelism and mixture-of-experts all-to-all are affordable, and it is why rack-scale systems, not chips, are now the unit of purchase.

**On memory.** LLM memory demand is growing faster than 1000% per year against HBM capacity growth under 50% per year. Model state alone is 16 bytes per parameter, so a 405B model needs 6.5 TB before a single activation — 81 H100s' worth. HBM is now the most expensive component on the package (~$2,900 of an estimated $6,400 B200 build cost, against ~$850 of logic silicon), and HBM4 pricing is why GPU rental prices *rose* about 40% between October 2025 and March 2026 even as packaging capacity expanded.

**On the competitive picture.** NVIDIA retains the strongest position, and the moat is breadth rather than any single benchmark: it was the only vendor submitting to all seven MLPerf Training v6.0 workloads. But the non-NVIDIA path is now demonstrably real at frontier scale, and it runs through vertically integrated hyperscaler silicon rather than through merchant competitors. Anthropic is the clearest revealed preference in the industry: roughly 500,000 Trainium2 chips at Project Rainier, up to one million TPUs contracted from October 2025, and multiple further gigawatts of TPU capacity from 2027 — a frontier lab running flagship models on two different non-NVIDIA platforms simultaneously. AMD has crossed from theoretically capable to shipping (Zyphra pretrained ZAYA1 on 1,024 MI300X), and its structural advantage is memory capacity per dollar, not compute.

**On price.** The provider tier moves the price more than the silicon generation does. An H100 costs 1.05 USD/hour on a marketplace and 12.29 USD/hour on Azure — a 12× spread for identical silicon, and 45× including spot. Google lists Ironwood at 12.00 USD per chip-hour while SemiAnalysis estimates Anthropic pays about 1.60. Any statement of the form "X is cheaper than Y" is a procurement claim, not a hardware claim.

**On what is actually scarce.** Not logic wafers. Advanced packaging (TSMC CoWoS, ~130k wafers/month in 2026 against roughly 1M wafers of annual demand), HBM stacks (all three suppliers sold out for 2026), and increasingly electricity and transformers. A GB200 NVL72 rack draws 132 kW and weighs 1.4 tonnes; a TPU v7 superpod draws nearly 10 MW.
